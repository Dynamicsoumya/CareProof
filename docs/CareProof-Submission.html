Home-care quality assurance and safety index

CareProof

From promise to proof.

A proposed, auditable standard for measuring whether care at home meets predefined safety and quality requirements. This document is a pilot submission. It is not a claim that the framework has been clinically validated.

Assessment date used in the simulated pilot: 28 September 2026.

All patients, caregivers, agencies, and devices in this document are fictional.

21 sources · 6 pillars · 4 safety gates · 8 simulated episodes

How to read this document

Three kinds of statement

Every claim below is one of three things. They are not mixed.

  • Established evidence. A statement tied to a named source. The source is listed in the evidence review and again in the references.
  • Interpretation. What this submission takes that source to mean for home care. The interpretation is ours.
  • Proposed framework. A weight, a threshold, a gate, or a score rule created for CareProof. These are implementation rules for a pilot. They are not medical facts, and they are not a validated early-warning score.
CareProof does not claim that a home episode which meets this standard is equivalent to hospital care. It claims something narrower: the episode can be measured, gated, and audited against a predefined standard.

01 · Executive summary

A high score is not hospital-grade.

Hospital-grade at home is often a promise. CareProof turns the promise into a 0–100 quality score, a set of safety gates that can veto that score, and a passport that keeps the evidence.

The proposed definition is strict. An episode meets the standard only when the score is at least 90 and every safety gate passes. A score of 75–89 with every gate passed is conditional compliance. A score below 75 with every gate passed requires improvement. Any failed gate means the episode is not compliant, whatever the score.

In the simulated fleet of eight episodes the mean score is 83. One episode meets the proposed standard. Five are not compliant. One of those five, Arun Patel, scores 94. His oxygen concentrator maintenance was due on 12 March 2026. The safety gate fails. The status is not compliant.

The research described here does not test whether CareProof improves outcomes. It specifies how the measure would be validated: content review by six to eight experts, a 30-episode feasibility and dual-assessment pilot, and only then a comparative study. A planning calculation for that later study, under stated assumptions, gives 111 episodes per group after 15% attrition. Those proportions are assumptions. They are not CareProof results.

02 · Problem

The proof stays in the hospital.

A hospital can show monitors, trained staff, equipment checks, an escalation path, and an audit trail. When the same person goes home, “hospital-grade” is often a phrase on a brochure. The core question of this submission is whether that phrase can be measured.

Home care is not a smaller hospital. Readings are irregular. The team may be one nurse and one attendant. A device lives in a bedroom, not a clinical-engineering workshop. Informal caregivers share the work. A standard that copies a ward checklist will fail the first quiet afternoon.

The submission therefore answers five practical questions.

  • Standards. What must “hospital-grade” home care include, as measurable pillars rather than slogans?
  • Equipment. When is a device eligible for use, and when must it be retired?
  • Caregivers. Which skills are observable, and what count is a pass?
  • Monitoring. How should a hospital early-warning chart be used when the data are sparse, without pretending the chart has been validated at home?
  • Proof. What study could show whether the standard works, without pretending this pilot is that study?

03 · Definition

What “hospital-grade at home” means here

Proposed definition. A home-care episode is hospital-grade, under this standard, only when two conditions are both true: the CareProof quality score is 90 or above, and every safety gate passes.

The score summarises six pillars. The gates cover deterioration response, equipment eligibility, critical caregiver skills, and infection or medication process. The gates are not extra points. They are vetoes.

This definition is configurable. The pillars, the gates, and the passport stay the same. Local contact paths, device law, and which early-warning chart is used as the reference scaffold can change. India is the worked example, using NABH care-home standards as context rather than as a copied checklist. A care home is not a home visit. No regulator has endorsed this prototype.

04 · Evidence review

Twenty-one sources, read for a design decision

Sources were chosen because each one changes a design decision: quality dimensions, equipment lifecycle, continuity, deterioration and response, infection and medication, the Indian residential-care context, or the statistics of validation. Sources were not collected to inflate a reference list.

The paragraphs below separate what the document is used for from what CareProof adds. They are not quotations presented as our own findings. Thresholds inside CareProof are proposed rules unless the row says they come from the cited chart.

S01 · Quality of care

WHO. Quality of care: a process for making strategic choices in health systems. Geneva: WHO; 2006.

What the source supports. WHO frames quality across safety, effectiveness, people-centredness, and the system dimensions needed to deliver them.

What CareProof proposes from it. CareProof uses separate pillars for clinical safety, process safety, continuity, and experience instead of one satisfaction score.

S02 · Quality systems

WHO. Handbook for national quality policy and strategy. Geneva: WHO; 2018.

What the source supports. A quality strategy needs measurement, governance, and an improvement loop. A slogan is not a system.

What CareProof proposes from it. The operating cycle is Measure → Score → Detect → Escalate → Audit → Improve.

S03 · People-centred care

WHO. Framework on integrated, people-centred health services. WHA69.24; 2016.

What the source supports. Services should be organised around people and coordinated across settings, including the home.

What CareProof proposes from it. The unit of assessment is a home-care episode, not a hospital bed day copied into a house.

S04 · Continuity

WHO. Continuity and coordination of care: a practice brief. Geneva: WHO; 2018.

What the source supports. When several formal and informal caregivers share a person, information has to travel with the care.

What CareProof proposes from it. Documentation is a pillar, and the CareProof Passport is the audit trail that carries the episode.

S05 · Equipment

WHO. Medical equipment maintenance programme overview. WHO Medical device technical series. Geneva: WHO; 2011.

What the source supports. A maintenance programme covers inspection, preventive maintenance, corrective maintenance, records, user error, and performance monitoring.

What CareProof proposes from it. A device is eligible for use only when inspection, maintenance, training, and incident status all pass.

S06 · Equipment inventory

WHO. Introduction to medical equipment inventory management. WHO Medical device technical series. Geneva: WHO; 2011.

What the source supports. Inventory is the base record for managing health technology: what the device is, where it is, and who is responsible.

What CareProof proposes from it. Every device keeps identity, dates, assigned patient, and training status.

S07 · Maintenance records

WHO. Computerized maintenance management system. WHO Medical device technical series. Geneva: WHO; 2011.

What the source supports. A CMMS holds service history, planned maintenance, and performance information rather than a paper sticker alone.

What CareProof proposes from it. Overdue maintenance is calculated from the next-due date. It is not a free-text impression.

S08 · Home and community care

WHO. Integrated care for older people (ICOPE): guidance on person-centred assessment and pathways in primary care. Geneva: WHO; 2019.

What the source supports. Older people need assessment and a care plan in primary and community settings, matched to their own function.

What CareProof proposes from it. HEWI compares a reading with the patient-specific baseline and with the previous reading.

S09 · Early warning

Royal College of Physicians. National Early Warning Score (NEWS) 2. London: RCP; 2017.

What the source supports. NEWS2 scores six physiological parameters in hospital, adds weight to supplemental oxygen on Scale 1, and uses Scale 2 when a target of 88–92% has been prescribed.

What CareProof proposes from it. Those bands are the reference scaffold for prototyping. HEWI is not presented as a validated home version of NEWS2.

S10 · Response to deterioration

NICE. Acutely ill adults in hospital: recognising and responding to deterioration. Clinical guideline CG50. London: NICE; 2007.

What the source supports. Track-and-trigger systems are incomplete without a graded response.

What CareProof proposes from it. A critical pattern fails the safety gate only when the required response does not happen. Biology and behaviour are separated.

S11 · Hospital alerting

NICE. National Early Warning Score systems that alert to deteriorating adult patients in hospital. MIB205. London: NICE; 2020.

What the source supports. NEWS2-based alerting is described for acute hospitals, with local agreement on the response.

What CareProof proposes from it. CareProof cites the hospital origin openly and does not claim the same tool has been shown to work on irregular home data.

S12 · Oxygen targets

O'Driscoll BR, Howard LS, Earis J, Mak V, on behalf of the BTS Emergency Oxygen Guideline Development Group. BTS guideline for oxygen use in adults in healthcare and emergency settings. Thorax. 2017;72:ii1–ii90.

What the source supports. Most acutely ill adults are targeted at 94–98%. People at risk of hypercapnic failure are targeted at 88–92%.

What CareProof proposes from it. A patient with a prescribed 88–92% target is scored on the Scale 2 scaffold so stable home oxygen is not treated as an emergency by itself.

S13 · Indian care-home standards

NABH. Accreditation Standards for Care Homes. 1st edition. September 2023.

What the source supports. The standards use six chapters, 23 standards, and 92 objective elements, covering management, facility safety, infection control, resident experience, rights, and staffing.

What CareProof proposes from it. India configuration points at this document. CareProof does not copy it: a care home is not the same setting as a home visit.

S14 · Indian accreditation context

NABH. Care Homes Accreditation Programme. https://nabh.co/programmes/care-homes-accreditation-programme/

What the source supports. NABH operates a voluntary accreditation programme for operational care homes and related residential services.

What CareProof proposes from it. Shows that an Indian quality body already treats residential care safety as standardisable. It is context, not the measurement model.

S15 · Infection prevention

WHO. Guidelines on core components of infection prevention and control programmes at the national and acute health care facility level. Geneva: WHO; 2016.

What the source supports. IPC programmes depend on observed practice, training, and monitoring, not only on a written policy.

What CareProof proposes from it. The infection pillar scores hygiene and PPE as episode-level process measures.

S16 · Medication safety

WHO. Medication Without Harm: WHO Global Patient Safety Challenge. Geneva: WHO; 2017.

What the source supports. Medication harm is a global patient-safety priority, and transitions between settings are a known weak point.

What CareProof proposes from it. A missed reconciliation or a broken five-rights step is a safety gate, not a small deduction inside a large score.

S17 · Content validity

Lynn MR. Determination and quantification of content validity. Nursing Research. 1986;35(6):382–385.

What the source supports. Content validity can be quantified from expert ratings of item relevance.

What CareProof proposes from it. Phase 2 uses an expert panel and a content-validity index before any effectiveness claim.

S18 · Content validity index

Polit DF, Beck CT. The content validity index: are you sure you know what's being reported? Research in Nursing & Health. 2006;29(5):489–497.

What the source supports. Item-level and scale-level CVI should be reported explicitly, with the number of experts visible.

What CareProof proposes from it. With six raters, an item is proposed for retention when at least five rate it relevant.

S19 · Reliability sample size

Walter SD, Eliasziw M, Donner A. Sample size and optimal designs for reliability studies. Statistics in Medicine. 1998;17(1):101–110.

What the source supports. The number of subjects needed for an inter-rater reliability study depends on the number of raters, the null and alternative ICC, alpha, and power.

What CareProof proposes from it. Phase 4 is specified as a two-assessor design. This pilot does not invent an ICC or claim the reliability study has been done.

S20 · Pilot sample size

Julious SA. Sample size of 12 per group rule of thumb for a pilot study. Pharmaceutical Statistics. 2005;4(4):287–291.

What the source supports. Twelve participants per group is discussed as a rule of thumb for estimating a variance, not as proof of an effect.

What CareProof proposes from it. Used only as context for estimating assessment time. It is not the sample size for an effectiveness claim.

S21 · Feasibility studies

Billingham SAM, Whitehead AL, Julious SA. An audit of sample sizes for pilot and feasibility trials. BMC Medical Research Methodology. 2013;13:104.

What the source supports. Published pilots often give a weak or missing justification for their sample size.

What CareProof proposes from it. The protocol separates a 30-episode feasibility pilot from the later comparative study and shows the arithmetic for that later study.

05 · Framework

Measure, then refuse a pretty number

The operating cycle is Measure, Score, Detect, Escalate, Audit, Improve. That cycle follows the idea, in the WHO quality-policy handbook, that a quality system needs measurement and an improvement loop. The original contribution is the split between the score and the gate.

Six proposed pillars

Weights sum to 100%. They are a design proposal for content validation. They were not fitted to outcomes.

PillarWeightWhy this weight is proposed
Clinical safety and monitoring25%WHO treats safety as part of quality. Missed deterioration is the acute harm this pillar is built to see. The response gate, not the weight, is the veto.
Caregiver competence20%At home the team is small. Recognition and escalation are also a gate, so a zero cannot hide inside the 20%.
Equipment safety15%WHO’s maintenance series treats devices as a lifecycle. The weight is modest because a failed device is already a gate.
Infection and medication safety15%WHO’s IPC guidance and the Medication Without Harm challenge both treat process as safety. A critical breach is a gate.
Documentation and continuity10%The record is how a distributed team coordinates. A missed escalation is already a response-gate failure, so the weight is lower.
Patient and family experience15%People-centred care is a quality dimension. It cannot talk a failed oxygen check into a pass.

Clinical safety and monitoring — indicators

  • Repeated home observations, not a single set.
  • A patient-specific baseline.
  • NEWS2 bands used only as a labelled reference scaffold (RCP 2017).
  • Deterioration steps between consecutive readings. This rule is proposed.

Caregiver competence — indicators

  • Seven observable domains, each scored 0 to 3.
  • Pass = at least 14 out of 21, no domain at zero, and both deterioration recognition and escalation at least Competent (2).
  • The assessment has a date and is then applied to the episode.

Equipment safety — indicators

  • Inspection in date. Weight inside the device score: 22%.
  • The named caregiver is certified for this device. 18%.
  • Maintenance in date. 25%.
  • Calibration or verification where the device measures. 15%.
  • Not retired. 10%. No open critical device incident. 10%.
  • Proposed cap. A failed inspection limits the equipment pillar to 45, because paperwork cannot make an uninspected device look safe.

Infection and medication — indicators

Hand hygiene, PPE for the task, medication reconciliation, the five-rights process, and storage. Each is scored 0–100 and averaged. A critical breach caps the pillar at 40 and fails the gate.

Documentation — indicators

Completeness of the required record, a current care plan, and an escalation note when a response was required.

Experience — indicators

Dignity, communication, pain relief, and caregiver burden reported by the family. Averaged to 0–100.

06 · Scoring and the safety gate

Layer 1 is a number. Layer 2 can veto it.

Each pillar is scored 0–100. The episode score is the weighted sum, rounded to an integer. The weights are the six percentages above.

ScoreGatesFinal status
90–100All passMeets the proposed standard
75–89All passConditional compliance
Below 75All passImprovement required
AnyAny failNot compliant

The four gates

  • Deterioration response. If the Home Early Warning Index is “repeated deterioration” or an “emergency pattern”, escalation must be logged and acknowledged inside the timer. If no such pattern occurred, the gate passes. A deterioration that was recognised and escalated lowers the clinical pillar. It does not, by itself, fail the gate. Missing the response does. This split follows the point of NICE CG50: a track-and-trigger score without a response is incomplete. Applying that point to home care is our interpretation.
  • Equipment eligible for use. Every assigned device must pass inspection, caregiver certification for that device, maintenance, calibration where required, in-service status, and incident status.
  • Caregiver critical skills. Deterioration recognition and escalation must both be at least Competent, and no domain may be zero.
  • Infection and medication process. No critical breach.

07 · Clinical monitoring

Home Early Warning Index

Reference scaffold, not a home-validated score. Heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, temperature, consciousness, and oxygen saturation are scored with the NEWS2 chart (Royal College of Physicians, 2017). Scale 2 is used only when a target of 88–92% is already prescribed, which is the logic of that chart and of the BTS oxygen guideline (2017). This submission does not claim those bands have been validated on irregular home data.

Proposed home rules, labelled as implementation rules.

  • A deterioration step is a worsening between two consecutive readings: oxygen saturation down by 2 points or more, heart rate up by 10 or more, respiratory rate up by 3 or more, temperature up by 0.4°C or more, systolic pressure down by 12 or more, or consciousness leaving Alert.
  • Stable: reference total 0 and no step.
  • Abnormal value: reference total 1–4, or a single step, without an emergency pattern.
  • Repeated deterioration: three or more steps, or a reference total of 5 or 6.
  • Emergency pattern: reference total 7 or more, consciousness not Alert, or a single parameter at the top of the reference chart plus at least two steps.

The clinical pillar starts at 100 and subtracts for the latest reference total and for deterioration steps, then applies a cap: 84 if abnormal, 64 if repeated deterioration, and 56 if an emergency pattern. The cap keeps the other pillars visible. The gate, not a zero, carries the non-negotiable decision about response.

The question is not only “is 88% dangerous?”. The question is “is the person deteriorating?”. Fatima Khan’s saturation moves 96, 94, 91, 88 across one day. That pattern is an emergency. Her nurse escalates, so the response gate passes. The concentrator inspection is overdue, so the episode still fails.

08 · Equipment

A device is a lifecycle, not a sticker

WHO’s medical equipment maintenance overview (2011) covers inspection, preventive and corrective maintenance, records, user error, and performance monitoring. The inventory and CMMS papers in the same technical series make the register and the next-due date the base record. The twelve stages below are a proposed operational chain aligned to that logic. They are not a diagram copied from a WHO page.

StageWhat it means in a home
01SelectThe clinical need and the home environment decide the device class.
02ProcurePurchase against that specification, with service access included.
03ReceiveCheck the delivery against the order before it reaches a patient.
04InspectA competent person confirms the device is safe to put into service.
05RegisterIdentity, serial number, owner, and location enter the register.
06Train userThe named caregiver is signed off on this device, not on the device class in general.
07UseThe device is assigned to one episode and one responsible caregiver.
08MonitorFaults, hours of use, and user-reported problems are captured.
09MaintainPreventive maintenance happens before the due date.
10Calibrate / verifyMeasurement devices are verified on a schedule.
11Incident reviewFailures feed a cause review, not only a repair ticket.
12RetireThe device is removed from eligible stock and the register says why.

Eligibility is a chain, not a pile of ticks. Inspection valid, then this caregiver certified for this device, then maintenance current, then calibration where the device measures, then still in service, then no open critical incident. Any break makes the device ineligible. A competent nurse cannot rescue an overdue concentrator.

Each device record holds an identifier, type, manufacturer, model, serial number, date received, last and next maintenance, last and next inspection, calibration status, assigned episode, trained caregivers, incident history, and retirement status. In a later build these fields are the PostgreSQL tables in server/schema.sql. This pilot calculates eligibility from the dates.

10 · Passport

Show the evidence trail

Every episode keeps a CareProof Passport: care plan, caregiver competency, equipment readiness, clinical monitoring, incidents, escalations, outcome, and the audit trail. A later repair does not erase the assessment that failed. The record is append-only.

The same core can sit under local rules. India uses NABH care-home standards as context for residential safety, infection control, staffing, and resident experience, and uses a local escalation contact. The United Kingdom can keep NEWS2 as the reference scaffold. The United States can slot local device and documentation rules into the same gates. These are configuration slots. They are not approved crosswalks.

11 · Research protocol

This pilot asks if the measure can be used

Question for a later study. Does implementation of the CareProof Standard improve measurable home-care safety and quality indicators compared with baseline care processes?

That question is not answered here. The protocol below is a content-validity, reliability, and feasibility study. It is not powered for effectiveness, and this submission reports no effectiveness result.

PhaseWhat happens
1. DevelopmentMap the sources. Separate evidence, interpretation, and proposal. Freeze the indicator list before anyone scores a case.
2. Content validationA panel of six to eight people: physician, nurse, biomedical engineer, public-health researcher, home-care professional, and a caregiver or family representative. Each item is rated for relevance.
3. Pilot casesThirty simulated or fully anonymised episodes, built to stress each gate. The eight cases in this document are a demonstration, not that sample.
4. ReliabilityTwo assessors score each episode independently. Report an intraclass correlation with a confidence interval. Do not claim reliability before that is done.
5. FeasibilityAssessment time, missing data, completion rate, caregiver burden, and whether a supervisor can act on a failed gate the same day.
6. RefinementChange a weight, a band, or a gate only when the panel or the pilot gives a reason. Publish the change.

Ethics and consent

  • This document contains no real patient, caregiver, or device.
  • A study on real episodes needs institutional ethics approval before recruitment.
  • Consent must be written, specific, and separate from the decision to receive care. Refusal does not change the care offered.
  • If a person cannot consent, the legally authorised representative decides, and the person’s wishes are still sought.
  • Anyone can withdraw. Data already required for a safety review are handled under the approval.
  • A live pilot needs a clinical escalation path. The study cannot watch an emergency pattern and only write it down.
  • Competency assessment is scheduled and consented. It is not covert surveillance of a home visit.

Analysis, once data exist

  • Content validity: item-level and scale-level indices, with the number of experts stated (Lynn 1986; Polit and Beck 2006). With six raters, an item is proposed for retention when at least five rate it relevant.
  • Reliability: a two-way intraclass correlation and a confidence interval, plus percent agreement on each gate. Walter, Eliasziw and Donner (1998) is the method for sizing a confirmatory reliability study once a realistic correlation is known. This pilot does not report an intraclass correlation, because no dual rating has been done.
  • Feasibility: median assessment time, proportion of complete episodes, missing indicators, and a short usability rating.
  • No p-value for “CareProof works” will be reported from the feasibility pilot.

12 · Sample size

Two numbers. They are not interchangeable.

This pilot

Content panel of 6–8 experts. Feasibility target of 30 dual-assessed episodes, so that missingness, time, and a preliminary intraclass correlation can be estimated. Thirty is not claimed as the optimum from Walter, Eliasziw and Donner (1998). Julious (2005) discusses 12 per group as a rule of thumb for estimating a variance. It is cited only for the assessment-time estimate. It is not an effectiveness sample size. Billingham, Whitehead and Julious (2013) show that published pilots often give a weak reason for their size. The split below is the reason used here.

Later comparative study — planning only

Primary endpoint: the proportion of episodes that meet the proposed standard (score at least 90 and every gate passed). Planning assumptions, not observed baselines: 40% without CareProof and 60% with it, a 20-point absolute difference treated as the smallest change worth a service decision. Two-sided alpha 0.05. Power 80%. Equal groups. Normal approximation to the difference of two proportions.

n = (Zα + Zβ)² × [p1(1−p1) + p2(1−p2)] / (p1 − p2)²
Zα = 1.96, Zβ = 0.8416, p1 = 0.40, p2 = 0.60
n = (2.8016)² × (0.24 + 0.24) / 0.04
n = 7.849 × 0.48 / 0.04 = 94.2 per group
Allow 15% attrition: 94.2 / 0.85 = 110.8, rounded up to 111 per group, 222 in total.

If the content review or the feasibility pilot shows that the measure is the wrong measure, this calculation is discarded. A precise estimate of a bad measure is not a success.

17 · Limits and what happens next

What this submission does not show

  • The weights are proposed. They are not derived from outcome regression.
  • NEWS2 bands are a hospital chart. Using them at home is a scaffold, not a validation.
  • Eight simulated episodes cannot estimate sensitivity, specificity, or effect.
  • No inter-rater result exists, so none is reported.
  • Local regulatory examples are configuration slots, not endorsements.
  • CareProof is not a medical device clearance and must not be used to withhold care.

Roadmap

Freeze the indicator list. Run the expert panel. Only then score the 30-episode feasibility set with two assessors. Publish missingness, time, agreement on gates, and a confidence interval for the intraclass correlation. Change the standard in public if the panel or the pilot requires it. A comparative study is justified only after that. The arithmetic for that study is in section 12, and it stays labelled as a plan.

Conclusion

A hospital can prove its care. A home usually cannot. CareProof is a proposed way to measure the proof: six pillars, one score, four gates that can veto the score, and a passport that keeps the trail. The simulated pilot shows why the veto matters. A score of 94 can still be not compliant. The framework is ready to be challenged. It is not ready to be called validated.
